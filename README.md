# The Crown as a Constitutional Relationship

## Dissertation foundation

**Working subtitle:** Treaty Federalism, Indigenous Sovereignty, the Honour of the Crown, and Evidence-Informed Governance in Twenty-First-Century Canada

This project develops a research framework for examining the Canadian Crown not only as a monarchical office or executive institution, but as a set of constitutional relationships among federal and provincial governments, Indigenous nations, Parliament, courts, and treaty partners. It takes *The Evolving Canadian Crown*, edited by Jennifer Smith and D. Michael Jackson, as a point of departure. The dissertation's argument, analysis, and proposed governance model remain the researcher's responsibility and require independent development and verification.

## Research problem and central proposition

Canadian constitutional scholarship often considers the Crown through separate lenses, including monarchy, executive authority, federalism, treaty relations, and section 35 jurisprudence. This dissertation asks whether these subjects can be understood together as parts of a continuing, relational constitutional architecture.

**Principal research question:** How should the concept and practice of the Crown be understood where Canadian federalism, section 35 Aboriginal and treaty rights, Indigenous legal orders, the honour of the Crown, democratic accountability, and reconciliation intersect?

**Central proposition to investigate:** The Crown is a distributed constitutional relationship, not solely a ceremonial institution or source of executive authority. Its legitimacy in contemporary Canada depends in part on how its institutions give practical effect to constitutional and treaty obligations, Indigenous self-determination, and accountable governance.

This is a proposition to test against primary sources, legal doctrine, historical evidence, and Indigenous perspectives—not an assertion that Canadian law has already adopted a single, unified theory of the Crown.

## Research questions

1. How do the Crown's legal, executive, federal, symbolic, and treaty-related roles relate to one another?
2. How should Crown obligations be understood when governmental powers are divided between federal and provincial institutions?
3. Does the honour of the Crown operate only as a judicial doctrine, or does it also support an institutional account of continuing treaty governance?
4. What processes could make treaty obligations, rights recognition, consultation, evidence, reasons, and accountability visible in government decision-making?
5. Where do Canadian constitutional law, treaty relationships, and Indigenous legal and political perspectives converge or differ?

## Proposed contribution

The dissertation will develop and assess a Treaty-centred account of the Crown that treats federalism, Indigenous constitutionalism, section 35, the honour of the Crown, and public administration as interacting subjects. It will then propose a governance framework for evaluating how institutions address those relationships in practice.

The proposed **E³ framework**—Evidence, Ethics, and Equity—is a researcher-developed analytical tool, not established constitutional doctrine. Any indicators or index derived from it will need clear definitions, a defensible methodology, and validation against appropriate evidence.

## Method and analytical distinctions

The project proposes a mixed doctrinal, historical, and institutional approach, supplemented by Indigenous methodologies and qualitative research where appropriate approvals and research relationships permit. Potential methods include analysis of legislation and judgments, archival research, treaty interpretation, institutional analysis, and a bounded Treaty 6 case study.

The analysis will distinguish throughout:

- **Established law:** what legislation and binding or persuasive decisions say, with jurisdiction and date identified.
- **Historical interpretation:** claims grounded in historical records and scholarship, including limits or conflicts in the evidence.
- **Indigenous legal and political perspectives:** attributed to the relevant nation, knowledge holder, or source, and not treated as interchangeable.
- **Researcher's analysis:** interpretations, hypotheses, and proposed models advanced by this project.

## Archival and editorial integrity

The project follows this working policy: **preserve original evidence; standardize modern analysis; document every substantive alteration.**

- Preserve the wording, spelling, and punctuation of historical legislation, treaty quotations, and judicial decisions in transcriptions. Mark editorial additions or omissions clearly and retain a link or citation to the source.
- Use consistent modern editorial conventions, including the Oxford comma, in the researcher's own prose. Do not silently modernize quoted or transcribed material.
- Record source title, creator, date, repository or stable URL, access date where relevant, and page or archival reference.
- Verify quotations and legal citations against primary sources wherever available; distinguish a source's own claims from the researcher's description of them.
- Maintain version history for transcriptions and substantive edits so that corrections are traceable.

## Source and citation practice

The 2012 collection *The Evolving Canadian Crown*, edited by Jennifer Smith and D. Michael Jackson, is a starting point rather than a sufficient evidentiary foundation. The research should build from verified primary materials—including legislation, judgments, treaty texts, archival records, and official reports—and engage relevant Indigenous-authored scholarship and nation-specific sources.

Legal citations should follow the McGill Guide (Canadian Guide to Uniform Legal Citation), subject to confirmation of the institution's requirements. Scholarly sources should use one consistently applied style, such as Chicago, where legal citation rules do not govern. Bibliographic details, quotations, and claims must be checked against the cited source before submission.
